Our latest update from Sudan reports that the seven wells remain intact, but their pumps are broken and need replacing. Our proposed next step is to restore pumping and build a reservoir, creating a water supply for growing and practical training.

Latest reported status · 27 October 2026

Seven existing wells reported intact. Pumps reported broken and requiring replacement. A technical inspection and water tests are still needed.

Our proposed direction

Sunlight into water. Water into growth.

Our proposed routine is to pump during daylight, when solar power is available, and fill a new reservoir for the planned vegetable greenhouses. Battery storage would support agreed electrical needs outside daylight hours; its capacity and connected equipment remain to be designed.

How much water should we store?

There is no single standard commercial greenhouse size. For this comparison only, imagine ten greenhouses measuring 10 × 50 metres each: 5,000 m² of total floor area. These dimensions have not been selected for the farm.

What does the earlier barrel calculation tell us?

Assuming one barrel holds approximately 200 litres, filling it in ten minutes gives 1.2 m³/hour per well. If all seven sustained that rate together, the combined historical rate would be 8.4 m³/hour. Twelve hours would give 100.8 m³; this is not a current test or a solar output forecast.

For example only, six equivalent hours at that full combined flow would give 50.4 m³ (50,400 litres). Six hours is an arithmetic input, not a prediction of local sunshine or pump performance. Solar output varies through the day. Confirm the barrel capacity and gallon standard, test all wells, and model the solar system before setting a daily supply figure.

Could a 100,000-litre reservoir be 10 × 5 × 2 metres?

That rectangular water volume equals 100 m³, or 100,000 litres. This is a volume illustration only, not an excavation or construction specification. A real reservoir needs allowance for unusable storage, freeboard, any sloping sides and lining, plus a suitable ground assessment, overflow, drainage and safe access. Its overall dimensions would therefore differ.

Next design step: confirm greenhouse sizes and a seasonal crop plan; measure sustainable well yield and pumping water levels; then calculate peak daily demand, refill performance and the agreed reserve. Specify reservoir geometry, pumps, solar panels, batteries and irrigation pressure as one coordinated system.

Restore the water supply.

Inspect the wells, test water quality and sustainable yield, then specify suitable replacement pumps, controls and power. Pump selection depends on the operating water level, required flow and delivery pressure.

Store water for when it is needed.

Develop a lined reservoir or suitable tank storage, with protection against contamination and safe access. Its size, lining and any cover will follow the water assessment and crop needs.

Connect the greenhouses.

Use a main pipe with controlled branches to the planned greenhouses. Filtered drip lines would supply water close to plant roots, with each irrigation zone controlled separately.

Pumping and irrigation can run at different times

Store first. Irrigate when the crops need it.

We plan to use solar-powered replacement pumps during daylight to fill the reservoir, replacing the earlier proposed 6 pm to 6 am pumping routine. Stored water would then be available for irrigation when crops need it, subject to the designed pressure supply.

Battery storage is also proposed. Its size will follow the agreed loads and operating hours, including any irrigation booster or controls needed outside daylight. It is not yet specified to run all seven well pumps overnight. Gravity supply or an irrigation booster will depend on site levels and pressure.

Why explore solar power?

Make use of Sudan’s sunshine.

The Global Solar Atlas reports a countrywide range of approximately 5.5–6.7 kWh/m² per day for long-term average global horizontal solar irradiation. This is sunlight energy reaching a horizontal surface, not electricity produced by a panel or guaranteed pump operating hours.

The farm’s solar design will use its exact location and monthly conditions, with allowances for heat, dust, shading and system losses. Sustainable well yield and the required pumping head must also be measured before choosing pumps, panel capacity or batteries.

A system designed for this farm

The right design. The skills to keep it working.

We aim to connect water supply, suitable energy and practical training in one coordinated system. The design would follow the farm’s land, crops, water quality and operating needs, and could expand as production develops.

Understand the water.

Test the well water, including salinity, and assess the soil and crops before choosing irrigation equipment. Filtration would protect drip lines; any additional water treatment would depend on the test results.

Match energy to the work.

Assess renewable power alongside available reliable supplies. Compare pumping schedules, energy use, operating costs and maintenance so the chosen system suits local conditions.

Build local skills.

Include training in pump operation, filter cleaning, irrigation controls and routine checks. Agree servicing, spare parts and clear maintenance responsibilities before handover.

What needs confirming before we request equipment?

Well condition and operating water levels; sustainable pumping rates and interaction between wells; water quality; greenhouse dimensions and crops; daily irrigation demand; storage capacity; site elevations; pipe routes; power availability; security, maintenance and a costed first-stage plan. The reported well depth of approximately 500 feet does not by itself determine pump size or water output.

The earlier barrel-filling account is historical information, not a current pump test or a confirmed supply for ten greenhouses.
